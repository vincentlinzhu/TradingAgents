- **Q1 2026 operating income:** **-$128.0M**
- **Q1 operating margin:** **~ -32.1%**
- **TTM EBITDA:** **-$38.6M**
- **Q1 2026 free cash flow:** **-$214.9M**

Yes, operating losses improved from **-$250M in Q4 2025** to **-$128M in Q1 2026**. That is progress. But for a company valued near **$59B**, “less negative” is not enough.

The bull case asks investors to pay a dominant-platform valuation before NBIS has proven dominant-platform economics.

---

## 3. Gross margin is encouraging, but AI infrastructure is not software

You keep emphasizing the **74% Q1 gross margin**. I agree it is impressive.

But gross margin is not the same as operating leverage, and it is definitely not the same as free cash flow. This business has a heavy cost structure that comes after gross profit:

- depreciation,
- GPU replacement cycles,
- power,
- cooling,
- networking,
- maintenance,
- financing costs,
- utilization risk,
- and potential pricing pressure.

Depreciation is already accelerating:

- **Q1 2025 D&A:** $49.1M  
- **Q1 2026 D&A:** $212.0M  

That is more than a fourfold increase in a year — and this is before the full burden of the planned **$20B–$25B 2026 capex program** flows through the model.

So yes, 74% gross margin is a good starting point. But the market is not paying for a good starting point. It is paying for the conclusion that those gross margins will survive scale, competition, depreciation, financing costs, and asset refresh cycles. That conclusion is not proven.

---

## 4. “Capex is the moat” is the most dangerous bull assumption

This is where I think the bull case is most vulnerable.

NBIS reportedly raised 2026 capex plans to **$20B–$25B**.

Put that against the current business:

- **TTM revenue:** $877.9M  
- **Q1 annualized revenue:** ~$1.6B  
- **Q1 2026 capex:** $2.47B  
- **2026 capex plan:** $20B–$25B  

That capex plan is more than **20x TTM revenue**.

You say capacity is the product. Fair. But in capacity businesses, overbuilding is always the risk. Scarcity today does not guarantee scarcity tomorrow. Capital is flooding into AI infrastructure from hyperscalers, CoreWeave, other neoclouds, sovereign AI projects, and enterprise internal buildouts.

That creates a classic capacity-cycle risk:

1. Shortage appears.
2. Capital floods in.
3. Supply expands.
4. Customers gain leverage.
5. Pricing pressure emerges.
6. Returns compress.

AI compute may be a fantastic market. But fantastic markets can still produce poor shareholder returns if too much capital chases the same opportunity.

Capex is only a moat if it earns high returns. Otherwise, it is just leverage attached to depreciating assets.

---

## 5. Deferred revenue proves demand — not returns

The **$4.78B deferred revenue** balance is a positive. I am not dismissing it.

But the bull is treating it as if it de-risks the entire buildout. It does not.

Deferred revenue does not tell us:

- contract margins,
- customer concentration,
- cancellation terms,
- renegotiation risk,
- timing of revenue recognition,
- required capex to fulfill obligations,
- or whether returns exceed depreciation and financing costs.

And compare the numbers:

- **Deferred revenue:** ~$4.78B  
- **2026 capex plan:** $20B–$25B  

Deferred revenue covers only a fraction of the planned capital commitment.

So yes, it reduces the “empty data center” risk. But it does not prove the coming infrastructure spend will generate attractive per-share returns.

## 6. Liquidity is strong today, but the balance sheet is becoming much riskier

You point to the cash balance, and that is fair:

- **Cash and equivalents:** $9.30B  
- **Current assets:** $11.24B  
- **Working capital:** $9.89B  
- **Current ratio:** 8.33  

NBIS is not distressed today.

But the other side matters:

- **Total debt:** $9.50B  
- **Debt/equity:** 132.43%  
- **Debt one year ago:** $187.8M  

Debt has gone from under **$200M** to nearly **$10B** in a year.

And with a **$20B–$25B capex plan**, the current cash balance does not fully fund the ambition. That means more capital is likely needed: debt, equity, convertibles, customer financing, asset-level financing, or strategic capital.

You call that optionality. I call it financing risk.

The next capital raise may come with costs: dilution, higher interest burden, restrictive terms, or economics that benefit customers and creditors as much as common shareholders.

## 7. Dilution is not a footnote

Diluted shares rose from:

- **237.9M in Q1 2025**
- to **309.0M in Q1 2026**

That is roughly a **30% increase** in one year.

You argue revenue per share improved despite dilution. True. But common shareholders ultimately need free cash flow per share, not revenue per share.

If NBIS keeps issuing equity-linked capital to fund this buildout, shareholders may find that revenue growth accrues to suppliers, lenders, customers, and new investors — not fully to existing holders.

That is especially important because NBIS is still free-cash-flow negative. The business has not yet proven it can self-fund growth.

## 8. Operating cash flow is not clean enough to lean on

The bull highlights **$2.26B in Q1 operating cash flow**.

But the report notes that working capital contributed about **$2.12B**, including a large “other working capital” benefit. That is not the same as recurring operating cash generation.

Meanwhile:

- **Latest four-quarter implied cumulative FCF:** roughly **-$3.15B**
- **Broader snapshot FCF:** **-$6.15B**

So NBIS is still not a self-funding compounder. It remains dependent on external capital and favorable financing conditions.

That is acceptable for an early-stage infrastructure buildout. It is not acceptable at any valuation.

## 9. Earnings quality is weak

Meanwhile, core operating income was **-$128.0M**.

That matters because the reported **89.6x TTM P/E** can make the stock appear less stretched than it really is. The **639.7x forward P/E** is a better signal that normalized earnings power remains thin relative to the stock price.

If bulls are not buying NBIS for GAAP earnings, fine. But then the company still needs to prove the operating business can generate real, recurring profitability. It has not done that yet.
