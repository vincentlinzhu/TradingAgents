I agree with the bull on one thing: ServiceNow is not a broken business. But that’s not the debate. The real question is whether investors are being paid enough to take the risk **right now**.

My answer is no.

The bull case leans heavily on quality, AI optionality, and forward growth assumptions. But the market is already aware ServiceNow is a high-quality enterprise software company. The problem is that the stock still requires a lot to go right at a time when the technicals are deteriorating, margins are under pressure, acquisition risk is rising, software multiples are fragile, and macro conditions are hostile to long-duration growth assets.

So I’d frame the bear case this way:

> `NOW` is a premium stock priced for continued execution, but the evidence is increasingly mixed. The downside risk from valuation compression, slowing forward indicators, margin pressure, AI monetization uncertainty, and a damaged chart outweighs the upside from a generic “quality compounder” argument.

# 1. The bull says the 30% drawdown creates opportunity. I say it may be the market repricing risk correctly.

A nearly **30% six-month decline** sounds attractive only if the stock has become cheap or if fundamentals are clearly accelerating. I don’t think either is obvious.

Yes, ServiceNow has pulled back. But even after that reset, the company still trades at:

- **63.7x trailing earnings**
- **9.4x book value**
- High-30s implied EV/EBITDA territory
- Only about a **4.6% free cash flow yield**

That is not distressed. That is not deep value. That is still a premium multiple for a company facing visible risks.

The bull points to the **forward P/E of 21.3x** and **PEG of 1.11** as evidence that valuation is reasonable. But those metrics are only as good as the estimates behind them. If revenue growth slips from low-20s toward high-teens, if margins fail to expand, or if acquisition integration costs continue weighing on profitability, that forward multiple can re-rate higher very quickly.

In other words, the bull case depends on estimates being right. The bear case only needs estimates to come down.

That asymmetry matters.

# 2. Growth is good, but the forward indicators are not as clean as the bull suggests

The bull highlights Q1 2026 revenue growth of **22.1% year over year**, and that is absolutely strong. But the market is forward-looking, and there are already reasons to question whether growth durability is as strong as the headline number suggests.

Current deferred revenue grew approximately **19.2% year over year**, while quarterly revenue grew **22.1%**. Total deferred revenue grew about **18.6%**.

That’s not bad, but it is a warning sign: deferred revenue growth is already running below reported revenue growth. For a subscription software company, deferred revenue is one of the key visibility indicators. If that gap persists, it may signal that future revenue growth is starting to moderate.

The bull says deal delays are “fine” because customers are still committing. But the market does not pay 60x trailing earnings for “fine.” It pays that multiple for consistent beats, clean visibility, accelerating adoption, and margin expansion.

Recent coverage has cited:

- **Deal delays**
- **Acquisition integration costs**
- **Competitive pressure**
- **Near-term outlook uncertainty**

Those are not imaginary risks. They are precisely the types of issues that cause premium software multiples to compress.

# 3. AI is not automatically a ServiceNow tailwind — monetization still has to be proven

The bull makes a big point that AI is a tailwind, not a threat. Maybe. But that is still more narrative than demonstrated financial evidence.

ServiceNow may be well positioned for enterprise AI workflows, but investors need to ask harder questions:

- Will AI features drive meaningful incremental revenue?
- Will customers pay more, or will AI become table stakes?
- Will AI reduce seat-based software demand over time?
- Will AI infrastructure costs pressure gross margins?
- Will competitors use AI to attack workflow automation at lower price points?
- Will large platforms like Microsoft, Salesforce, Atlassian, and others bundle competing workflow/agent capabilities into existing enterprise relationships?

The bull says ServiceNow can become the “operating layer” for enterprise AI-driven work. That sounds compelling, but every major enterprise software company is making some version of that same pitch.

- Higher average contract values
- Better net retention

Right now, the evidence is not definitive enough to justify aggressive buying.

# 4. The margin trend is a real problem, not just “investment from strength”

The bull downplays margin pressure by saying ServiceNow is investing for growth. That’s the standard bull explanation for every high-growth software company experiencing margin compression.

But the numbers matter.

Q1 2026 margins showed deterioration:

- Gross margin declined from roughly **78.9%** in Q1 2025 to **75.1%** in Q1 2026
- Operating margin declined from roughly **14.6%** to **13.3%**

That is not catastrophic, but it is directionally negative.

For a premium-valued software company, investors want operating leverage. They want proof that revenue growth is scaling into higher profitability. Instead, ServiceNow is spending heavily:

- R&D: **$823 million**, or **21.8% of revenue**
- Selling and marketing: **$1.22 billion**, or **32.3% of revenue**
- Total operating expenses: about **61.7% of revenue**

The company is still highly profitable, but the bear question is simple:

> If ServiceNow is already a nearly $14 billion revenue company, when does operating leverage become more consistent?

If the answer is “later,” then the stock deserves a lower multiple today.

# 5. Free cash flow is strong, but the quality of shareholder returns is less attractive than the bull admits

The bull points to Q1 free cash flow of **$1.53 billion** and a quarterly FCF margin of **40.6%**. That is impressive.

But let’s not ignore what happened with capital allocation.

In Q1 2026:

- Free cash flow was **$1.53 billion**
- Share repurchases were **$2.23 billion**
- Stock-based compensation was **$547 million**, or about **14.5% of revenue**

So the company spent more on buybacks than it generated in free cash flow during the quarter, while also issuing a material amount of stock-based compensation.

That raises two concerns.

First, buybacks may be supporting EPS optics rather than creating real long-term value, especially if shares are repurchased at elevated multiples.

Second, the buybacks are partly needed to offset dilution from SBC. That means some of the free cash flow bulls celebrate is effectively being recycled to manage dilution.

This is not fatal, but it weakens the “cash machine” narrative. A truly compelling free cash flow story should come with disciplined capital allocation and declining dilution, not rising buybacks that exceed quarterly FCF.
