RSI around 60 is not broken, but it is no longer showing the same upside force that existed when RSI was above 80. The aggressive analyst says waiting for confirmation means paying up. My response is simple: paying up after confirmation may be preferable to buying before confirmation and absorbing a sharp drawdown in a high-beta name.

The volatility problem remains underappreciated. ATR is around $77.65. From the current price near $1,043, a one-ATR move takes MU toward about $966, and a two-ATR move takes it toward about $888. That means the proposed $930 stop is not a clean risk boundary. It sits inside a plausible volatility band. Around earnings, semiconductor rotation, or a guidance disappointment, the stock could gap through $930 and move toward $891–$864 before the firm can exit cleanly.

So I reject the idea that this is “defined risk” in any practical sense. It is referenced risk, not contained risk.

The aggressive analyst’s upside/risk framing also remains too optimistic. Using the Deutsche Bank $1,500 target as the upside anchor while treating $930 as a manageable downside line creates a false sense of asymmetry. Price targets are scenarios, not risk controls. They are often raised after strong moves, when sentiment is already hot. Meanwhile, downside can accelerate if investors start questioning peak margins, forward EPS sustainability, DRAM/HBM pricing, or the durability of AI demand.

The valuation argument also needs discipline. Yes, the forward P/E near 9.1x and PEG around 0.32 look attractive if forward EPS of roughly $114.59 is achieved and sustained. But that “if” is the entire trade. On trailing metrics, MU is much less obviously cheap: trailing P/E around 49x and price/book around 16x. In cyclical semiconductors, stocks often look cheapest on forward numbers when earnings are near a cyclical high. AI/HBM may improve the cycle, but it does not eliminate that risk.

I am not dismissing the fundamental progress. Revenue up nearly 196% year over year, gross margin around 74%, operating margin near 68%, operating cash flow of $11.9 billion, free cash flow of $5.5 billion despite heavy capex, declining inventory, and cash above debt are all impressive. But for a risk manager, those numbers also raise the sustainability question. A 74% gross margin for a memory company is extraordinary. Extraordinary margins attract supply response, customer pushback, capacity additions, and eventual normalization. The market may be extrapolating best-case conditions too far forward.

The aggressive analyst says HBM is not old PC DRAM. Correct. But different does not mean non-cyclical. MU is still exposed to memory pricing, capex cycles, customer inventory behavior, semiconductor risk appetite, and macro-driven multiple compression. Latest-quarter capex was $6.387 billion. That is manageable while operating cash flow is booming. But if pricing weakens or demand visibility changes, free cash flow can compress quickly. In memory, capex can be a weapon in the upcycle and a burden if the cycle turns.

Receivables also should not be waved away. Receivables rose to $17.314 billion. That may be normal given revenue growth, but it is still a monitoring risk. When a company’s revenue and profitability accelerate this fast, we need to verify that cash conversion, customer quality, and demand durability remain strong. Declining inventory is positive, but it does not cancel the need to monitor receivables and potential demand pull-forward.

On sentiment, I think the aggressive analyst is much too comfortable. News, retail, and institutional sentiment are all highly bullish. Analyst upgrades are widespread. Stock-split chatter has appeared. MU is being discussed as a core AI infrastructure winner. That can fuel momentum, yes, but it also raises the bar. When sentiment is this positive, a merely good report can disappoint. The risk is not that MU has bad fundamentals. The risk is that very good fundamentals are already expected.

To the neutral analyst, I agree that the 50%–60% initial exposure is too aggressive. But I still think the proposed 20%–30% starter is unnecessary in the current zone. The neutral view admits the current area is not ideal: above support, below resistance, with elevated ATR and imperfect momentum. If that is true, why buy common stock here at all? A small starter may feel balanced, but it still introduces downside risk before either support confirmation or breakout confirmation.

Opportunity risk is real, but it should not dominate a conservative mandate. The firm’s objective is not to capture every breakout. It is to protect assets, minimize volatility, and compound reliably. If MU breaks above $1,110–$1,120 with strong volume and improving momentum, we can reassess. If it pulls back to $982–$977 and stabilizes without distribution, we can reassess. Those entries may not be perfect, but they are cleaner than buying in the middle of the range.

My recommended adjustment remains:

Hold existing MU exposure if already long, but manage it actively.

Do not approve the proposed BUY with 50%–60% initial exposure.

Do not add meaningful common-stock exposure in the $1,020–$1,090 range.

Consider partial profit-taking if MU fails again near $1,089–$1,120.

For new capital, wait for either a controlled pullback into $982–$977 that stabilizes, or a confirmed breakout above $1,110–$1,120 with strong volume and improving momentum.

If participation is mandatory, use a very small defined-risk structure, such as a call spread, rather than meaningful outright equity exposure.

And most importantly, size any position assuming a gap toward $891–$864 is possible, not assuming a clean exit at $930.

MU may continue higher. I am not denying the upside. But this is not a low-risk entry. It is a stretched, crowded, high-beta semiconductor leader sitting below resistance with weakening momentum and a stop that is too close relative to actual volatility. For a conservative risk process, the safest path is HOLD, preserve capital, and demand either a better entry or stronger confirmation before committing fresh firm capital.
